There are trips you plan and trips that happen to you. Morocco is the second kind. You think you are going to see a desert and eat some tagine. What actually happens is that the country rearranges your sense of what travel is supposed to feel like.

I went without a tight itinerary. That was the right call.

## Why Morocco Works as an Adventure Template

Most adventure trips ask you to choose: dramatic landscapes *or* cultural depth *or* physical challenge. Morocco refuses the trade-off. In a single week you can sleep under the Saharan stars, cross a high pass in the Atlas Mountains, lose yourself in a medina where the streets haven't changed in five hundred years, and eat the best food you've had all year — none of it planned, all of it earned.

That combination is rarer than it sounds. Most places that deliver on scenery are thin on culture. Most places rich in history are not particularly wild. Morocco stacks all three, and they sit close enough together that you move between them in hours, not days.

## The Desert

The Sahara arrives before you expect it. The road south from Ouarzazate starts as a wide valley, then the valley narrows, then the colour drains out of the landscape and suddenly there is only sand and silence and the particular quality of light that exists nowhere else.

Erg Chebbi, near Merzouga, is where most people enter the dunes. The scale takes time to absorb — the dunes are over 150 m high in places, and they shift. What you see on a map is not what you will find when you arrive. The desert is not static. It is doing something, slowly, in the direction of the wind.

The right way to experience it is to ride in on camelback at dusk, sleep in a camp in the dunes, and be up before the sun. The light at dawn in the Sahara is a thing I am still trying to find words for. Pink giving way to orange giving way to the hard blue of a desert sky, shadow cutting across the dunes in geometry that makes no sense until you are standing in it. The cold of the night not yet gone, the warmth already starting. That hour is worth the entire trip by itself.

## The Atlas Mountains

The High Atlas sits between the desert and the coast like a spine. Jebel Toubkal, at 4,167 m, is the highest peak in North Africa and one of the most accessible high-summit climbs on the continent. No technical skills required, no special equipment beyond warm layers and solid boots — just two days of hard walking.

The approach village is Imlil, a small Berber town at 1,740 m. From there the standard route climbs through the Mizane Valley to the Toubkal Refuge at 3,207 m, then a final push to the summit the next morning. The summit view on a clear day takes in the Atlas range in both directions, the desert to the south, and, on the best days, a haze at the horizon that might be the Atlantic.

The mountain is not technically demanding, but the altitude is real. Go slowly on the approach day. Drink more water than you think you need.

Beyond Toubkal, the Atlas offers weeks of trekking through Berber villages that receive almost no visitors — stone houses built into the hillside, terraced fields, the smell of woodsmoke in the afternoon. This is the Morocco most people miss, and it is worth finding.

## The Medinas

After the desert and the mountains, the medinas feel like a different planet — and that contrast is part of what makes Morocco work.

Fès el-Bali is the oldest and densest: 9,000 streets in a space of a few square kilometres, most of them too narrow for a car, many of them too narrow for two people to pass comfortably. It has been the same approximate shape since the 9th century. The tanneries are still here. The madrasas are still here. The call to prayer still moves across the rooftops in waves.

Getting lost in Fès is not a problem to solve — it is the point. Put your phone away. Walk toward noise. Follow your nose. At some point you will emerge somewhere unexpected, buy something you didn't know you wanted, eat lunch standing up at a stall you couldn't find again if you tried.

Marrakech is more polished, more touristy, more immediately photogenic. It is worth a day or two. But Fès is where you feel what the medinas actually are: a city that chose to remain itself.

## The Practicalities

**Getting around:** The most flexible approach is a combination of train between the major cities (Casablanca, Fès, Marrakech) and shared taxis or hired car for the mountains and desert. The train network is genuinely good. South of Marrakech, a car gives you freedom that nothing else does.

**When to go:** Spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November) are the right windows. Summer in the desert is brutal; the mountains are beautiful but the desert camps are punishing. Winter on Toubkal means snow and cold.

**How long:** Two weeks is the minimum to do it properly. One week means choosing between desert and mountains. Three weeks means doing both slowly, with time left over to sit in a riad and read.

**Food:** Eat at the stalls, not at the restaurants with photos on the menu. Harira soup. Msemen with honey. A proper tagine cooked for three hours. The food in Morocco is some of the best I've eaten anywhere, and most of it costs almost nothing.

## What It Teaches You

I said at the start that Morocco rearranges your sense of what travel is supposed to feel like. What I mean is this: most trips have a single register — beach holiday, city break, trekking trip. Morocco doesn't. It shifts constantly, asks you to be different kinds of traveller in the same week, and rewards the flexibility.

The perfect adventure trip, if there is such a thing, shouldn't just challenge you physically. It should surprise you. It should put you in rooms and landscapes and conversations you didn't anticipate, and leave you with the particular tiredness that comes from having actually been somewhere.

Morocco does all of that. Almost every day.
